What this calculator does, step by step

The calculator rebuilds a typical Indian payslip from the CTC you enter. Basic pay is taken as the percentage you set (40% by default). HRA is 50% of basic for the four metro cities and 40% elsewhere. The employer's PF contribution is 12% of basic and the gratuity accrual is 4.81% of basic. Employer ESI (3.25% of gross) is added only when monthly CTC is ₹21,000 or less. Special allowance is whatever is left of monthly CTC after those items, so the structure always adds up to your CTC.

Gross salary is basic plus HRA plus special allowance plus any monthly bonus you enter. From gross, the calculator deducts employee PF (12% of basic), the professional tax you enter, and an estimate of income tax under the new tax regime: a ₹75,000 standard deduction, the current slabs, the Section 87A rebate for taxable income up to ₹12 lakh (with marginal relief just above it) and 4% cess, spread over twelve months as TDS. What remains is your in-hand salary.

The components of a CTC

Paid to you every month

  • Basic pay: the base on which PF, gratuity and HRA are computed. Fully taxable.
  • Dearness allowance (DA): common in government and public sector pay, rare in private companies. Counts with basic for PF and gratuity.
  • House rent allowance (HRA): usually 40% to 50% of basic. Partly exempt under the old tax regime if you pay rent.
  • Special allowance: the balancing figure that makes the structure add up to CTC. Fully taxable.
  • Reimbursements and perks: meal coupons, leave travel allowance, phone or internet reimbursement, each with its own tax rule.

Inside CTC but not in your monthly bank credit

  • Employer PF: 12% of basic plus DA, credited to your EPF and EPS accounts.
  • Gratuity accrual: 4.81% of basic, payable only when you leave after five years.
  • Variable or performance pay: counted at target in the CTC, paid quarterly or yearly and only to the extent earned.
  • Insurance premiums: group health and life cover paid by the employer on your behalf.
  • Employer ESI: 3.25% of wages, only when gross is ₹21,000 a month or less (the employee pays 0.75%).
  • Employer NPS contribution: where offered, deductible under Section 80CCD(2) within the limits set in the Income Tax Act.

EPF and EPS: where the two 12% contributions go

You contribute 12% of basic plus DA to the Employees' Provident Fund, and your employer contributes another 12%. The employer's share is split: 8.33% goes to the Employees' Pension Scheme (EPS) and the rest to your EPF account. The EPS share is calculated on a wage ceiling of ₹15,000 a month, so it is capped at ₹1,250 a month; anything above that from the employer's 12% flows into EPF. The ₹15,000 ceiling is also the statutory minimum wage base for mandatory PF, which is why some employers contribute 12% of ₹15,000 rather than 12% of your full basic. This calculator assumes 12% of full basic on both sides, which many organised private employers follow.

Your own 12% counts toward the ₹1.5 lakh Section 80C limit under the old tax regime, and you can add a voluntary provident fund (VPF) contribution above 12% if you want more of your salary in EPF. EPFO declares the interest rate for each financial year; check the current rate on the EPFO website rather than relying on last year's figure.

Gratuity: the 4.81% explained

Under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, an employee who leaves after five years of continuous service is paid 15 days of last drawn basic plus DA for every completed year of service. Because the Act uses a 26-day working month, the formula is 15 divided by 26, multiplied by last drawn basic plus DA, multiplied by completed years. Spread over a year, 15/26 of one month's basic is 4.81% of basic per month, which is the accrual employers show in the CTC.

Example: last drawn basic plus DA of ₹40,000 a month and seven completed years gives 15/26 x 40,000 x 7 = ₹1,61,538. Gratuity received by employees covered by the Act is exempt from income tax up to ₹20 lakh over a working life. If you leave before completing five years, the accrued amount is not paid, so for a two-year stint the gratuity line in the CTC is money you will never see.

HRA exemption (old tax regime only)

Under Section 10(13A) and Rule 2A, the exempt part of HRA is the least of three amounts: the HRA you actually receive; rent paid minus 10% of basic plus DA; and 50% of basic plus DA if you live in Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata or Chennai, or 40% elsewhere. The rest of the HRA is taxable. The exemption is available only under the old regime; under the new regime HRA is fully taxable, which is why the city selector here changes only the size of the HRA line, not the tax estimate.

Example: basic ₹40,000 a month, HRA received ₹20,000, rent paid ₹15,000, metro city. The three figures are ₹20,000; ₹15,000 minus ₹4,000 = ₹11,000; and 50% of ₹40,000 = ₹20,000. The exempt HRA is ₹11,000 a month and ₹9,000 is taxed. Keep rent receipts, and if annual rent exceeds ₹1 lakh your employer will ask for the landlord's PAN.

New regime, old regime, and your take-home

The new tax regime, which this calculator uses, gives a ₹75,000 standard deduction and taxes income in slabs of 5% (₹4 lakh to ₹8 lakh), 10% (to ₹12 lakh), 15% (to ₹16 lakh), 20% (to ₹20 lakh), 25% (to ₹24 lakh) and 30% above that, with a Section 87A rebate that brings tax to zero when taxable income is ₹12 lakh or less. These slabs apply for FY 2025-26 and continue for FY 2026-27 as of September 2026. The old regime keeps a ₹50,000 standard deduction and older slabs (5% from ₹2.5 lakh, 20% from ₹5 lakh, 30% above ₹10 lakh) but allows HRA exemption, Section 80C, Section 80D and home loan interest deductions.

Take the ₹10 lakh example above. Under the new regime the taxable income is ₹8,57,760 and the tax is zero after the rebate. Under the old regime, with rent of ₹20,000 a month giving a full ₹2,00,000 HRA exemption and ₹1,50,000 of Section 80C investments (your ₹48,000 of employee PF counts toward it), taxable income is ₹5,32,760 and the tax is ₹19,814 including cess, about ₹1,651 a month. The new regime wins here. The old regime tends to come out ahead only when large deductions stack up, typically home loan interest plus HRA plus 80C plus 80D. Test your own numbers with the income tax calculator, which compares both regimes.

Reading and negotiating an offer letter

  • Ask for the fixed monthly gross, not just the CTC. Two offers with the same CTC can differ by thousands of rupees a month in hand.
  • Separate fixed from variable. Variable pay is counted at 100% in the CTC but is paid on performance; assume a partial payout when comparing.
  • Check what sits in retirals. Employer PF and gratuity are your money eventually, but they inflate the headline number and gratuity is forfeited before five years.
  • Watch for one-time items. Joining bonuses often carry a clawback if you leave within a year, and stock grants are usually shown at a notional value.
  • Insurance and perks have real value but are not cash. Ask whether the premium is inside the CTC.
  • Compare in-hand plus retirals across offers rather than the CTC figure alone.

Professional tax is levied by state governments under Article 276 of the Constitution and cannot exceed ₹2,500 a year. Each state sets its own monthly slabs, and several states and union territories do not levy it. Change the professional tax field above to match your state.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between CTC and in-hand salary?

CTC (cost to company) is the total yearly amount the employer books for you: gross salary plus the employer's PF contribution, the gratuity accrual, insurance premiums and often the target variable pay. In-hand salary is what reaches your bank each month after employee PF, professional tax and TDS are deducted from gross. With a standard structure, a ₹10 lakh CTC gives about ₹73,530 a month in hand, not ₹83,333.

How is in-hand salary calculated from CTC?

Monthly CTC minus the employer PF contribution and the gratuity accrual gives your gross salary. Gross minus employee PF (12% of basic), professional tax and estimated income tax (TDS) gives in-hand. With this calculator's default structure (40% basic, metro HRA, ₹200 professional tax, new tax regime), in-hand is about 88% of monthly CTC at ₹10 lakh, 83% at ₹15 lakh, 80% at ₹20 lakh and 69% at ₹50 lakh, because income tax rises with income.

Why is my in-hand salary so much less than the CTC I was offered?

Employer PF (12% of basic) and the gratuity accrual (4.81% of basic) sit inside the CTC but never reach your bank each month. Insurance premiums and variable pay counted at its target value inflate the CTC further. From the gross that remains, employee PF, professional tax and TDS are deducted. Each item is small on its own; together they commonly remove 12% to 30% of the CTC figure, more at higher incomes.

Should I ask for a higher or lower basic pay?

A higher basic raises both PF contributions and the gratuity accrual, which improves retirement savings but lowers monthly in-hand. A lower basic pushes more money into special allowance, which is fully taxable and increases take-home. HRA is usually set as a percentage of basic, so under the old tax regime with rent to claim, a higher basic also raises the HRA exemption ceiling. Many salaried structures set basic at roughly 40% to 50% of CTC.

What is the PF wage ceiling?

The statutory wage ceiling for mandatory EPF is ₹15,000 a month of basic plus dearness allowance, so the minimum employer contribution is 12% of ₹15,000. Of the employer's 12%, 8.33% (₹1,250 at the ceiling) goes to the Employees' Pension Scheme and the balance goes to your EPF account. Many companies contribute 12% of full basic, which this calculator assumes for both the employee and the employer. You can also contribute more than 12% from your side through the voluntary provident fund (VPF).

Does the calculator use the old or the new tax regime?

It estimates TDS under the new tax regime only: a ₹75,000 standard deduction, the slabs that apply for FY 2025-26 and FY 2026-27, the Section 87A rebate for taxable income up to ₹12 lakh with marginal relief just above it, and 4% cess. If you claim HRA, home loan interest or Section 80C deductions under the old regime, compare both regimes on our income tax calculator and use that figure in place of the estimate here.

Is gratuity really part of my salary?

It is part of your CTC but not of your monthly pay. Under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, gratuity becomes payable when you leave after five years of continuous service, at 15 days of last drawn basic plus DA for every completed year of service (the 15/26 formula). Employers therefore book 4.81% of basic every month as an accrual. If you leave before completing five years, the accrued amount is not paid, so for a short stint that slice of the CTC is notional.

What is professional tax and why does it vary by state?

Professional tax is a levy that state governments impose on salaried people and professionals under Article 276 of the Constitution, capped at ₹2,500 a year. Each state sets its own slabs, which is why Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana, West Bengal and Tamil Nadu deduct different monthly amounts, and some states and union territories, including Delhi, do not levy it at all. Your employer deducts it from salary, and under the old tax regime it is allowed as a deduction from salary income.
