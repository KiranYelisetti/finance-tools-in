Verdict

Which years does this calculator cover?

Both FY 2025-26 (the return you file in 2026) and FY 2026-27 (the year running from 1 April 2026). The Budget for FY 2026-27 left the new regime slabs, the Section 87A rebate and the standard deduction exactly where Budget 2025 put them, so one set of slabs serves both years. The rates and rules on this page were checked in September 2026.

One thing did change on 1 April 2026: the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act. It uses the term tax year in place of the old pair of previous year and assessment year, so FY 2026-27 is simply tax year 2026-27 under the new law. The slab structure, the rebate and the two standard deductions carried over unchanged.

The calculator assumes a resident individual below 60 with salary and, optionally, other income taxed at slab rates. It does not compute surcharge on income above ₹50 lakh, capital gains taxed at special rates, or the higher basic exemption that senior citizens get under the old regime.

How the ₹12 lakh rebate and marginal relief work

Under the new regime, Section 87A wipes out the tax when taxable income (gross salary minus the ₹75,000 standard deduction, plus any other income) is ₹12,00,000 or less. The tax on exactly ₹12 lakh works out to ₹60,000, which is why the rebate is capped at that figure. A salaried person with no other income therefore pays nothing up to a gross salary of ₹12,75,000.

Without a safety valve, earning one rupee over the limit would trigger the full ₹60,000 of tax. Marginal relief prevents that: just above ₹12 lakh, the tax payable cannot exceed the amount by which income exceeds ₹12 lakh. The relief tapers off at about ₹12,70,588 of taxable income, where the normal slab tax and the excess income become equal.

Worked example: taxable income of ₹12,10,000

  • Slab tax: ₹60,000 on the first ₹12 lakh plus 15% of ₹10,000 = ₹61,500
  • Income above ₹12 lakh: ₹10,000
  • Marginal relief caps the tax at ₹10,000, so ₹51,500 is rebated
  • Add 4% cess on ₹10,000: ₹400
  • Tax payable: ₹10,400, instead of ₹63,960 without relief

Two limits to remember. First, the rebate is for resident individuals only. Second, as of September 2026 it does not cover income taxed at special rates, such as short-term equity gains under section 111A or long-term equity gains under section 112A, so a person with an ₹11 lakh salary and ₹2 lakh of equity gains still pays tax on the gains even though the salary alone would be tax-free.

If you rent in a metro city, pay a home loan on another house and max out every section, the old regime can still be worth it; otherwise the new regime is usually the simpler and cheaper choice.

What you can still claim: new regime vs old regime

Allowed in the new regime

  • Standard deduction of ₹75,000 against salary or pension.
  • Employer NPS contribution under Section 80CCD(2), up to 14% of basic salary plus dearness allowance.
  • Family pension deduction of one-third of the pension, capped at ₹25,000.
  • Home loan interest on a let-out property under Section 24(b), set off only against that rental income.
  • Exemptions that are not deductions, such as gratuity and leave encashment within their limits, continue to apply.

Old regime only

  • Section 80C up to ₹1.5 lakh: EPF, PPF, ELSS, life insurance premiums, SSY, NSC, 5-year tax-saver FD, home loan principal, tuition fees.
  • Section 80CCD(1B): an extra ₹50,000 for your own NPS contribution.
  • Section 80D: health insurance, ₹25,000 for self and family (₹50,000 if a senior citizen) plus ₹25,000 or ₹50,000 for parents.
  • HRA exemption: the least of actual HRA received, rent paid minus 10% of basic plus DA, and 50% of basic plus DA in Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata or Chennai (40% elsewhere).
  • Section 24(b): up to ₹2 lakh of interest on a self-occupied home loan.
  • Others: education loan interest (80E, no cap, eight years), donations (80G), savings account interest up to ₹10,000 (80TTA, ₹50,000 for seniors under 80TTB), and leave travel allowance for two journeys in a block of four years.

Gross income, taxable income and tax payable

Gross total income is everything you earned in the year across the five heads: salary, house property, business or profession, capital gains and other sources such as interest. Taxable income (the law calls it total income) is what is left after the standard deduction and, in the old regime, the Chapter VI-A deductions. The slab rates apply to this figure. Tax payable is the slab tax minus any Section 87A rebate, plus surcharge where applicable, plus 4% cess. What you actually hand over at filing time is tax payable minus the TDS and advance tax already paid, and if that is negative you get a refund.

The effective tax rate shown by the calculator is total tax divided by gross income. It is always lower than your top slab rate because the lower slabs are taxed at lower rates, and it is the more honest number when you compare job offers or plan a raise.

TDS, advance tax and filing

An employer deducts tax at source on salary every month under section 192, using the regime you declare at the start of the year. The new regime is the default, so tell your payroll team if you want the old one, and ask for Form 16 in June to check the figures. You can still pick the other regime when you file the return, as long as you have no business income.

Advance tax applies when your tax after TDS exceeds ₹10,000 in a year, which usually happens only when you have sizeable interest, rent or capital gains. It is paid in four instalments: 15% by 15 June, 45% by 15 September, 75% by 15 December and 100% by 15 March. Shortfalls attract interest under sections 234B and 234C. Before filing, reconcile your TDS with Form 26AS and the Annual Information Statement. The return for individuals who do not need an audit is normally due on 31 July of the following year, though the CBDT sometimes extends the date by notification.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which tax regime is better, Old or New, for FY 2025-26 and FY 2026-27?

For most salaried people the new regime wins because of the ₹75,000 standard deduction and the Section 87A rebate that makes tax zero up to ₹12 lakh of taxable income. The old regime only wins when your deductions beyond the standard deduction are large: about ₹5.4 lakh at a ₹15 lakh salary, about ₹7.1 lakh at ₹20 lakh, and ₹8 lakh at ₹25 lakh or more. What is the standard deduction for FY 2025-26 and FY 2026-27?

The standard deduction is ₹75,000 under the new regime and ₹50,000 under the old regime in both years. It is available only against salary and pension income, needs no proof or investment, and cannot exceed the salary itself.

How does the ₹12 lakh rebate under Section 87A work?

Under the new regime, if your taxable income after the standard deduction is up to ₹12,00,000, Section 87A rebates the entire tax (up to ₹60,000), so a salary of up to ₹12,75,000 pays zero tax. Just above ₹12 lakh, marginal relief limits the tax to the amount by which your income exceeds ₹12 lakh, and the relief fades out at about ₹12.71 lakh of taxable income. As of September 2026 the rebate does not cover income taxed at special rates, such as capital gains under sections 111A and 112A.

Did Budget 2026 change the income tax slabs?

No. The Budget for FY 2026-27 kept the new regime slabs, the ₹60,000 rebate under Section 87A and the ₹75,000 standard deduction exactly as they were for FY 2025-26. The Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026 and uses the term tax year instead of previous year and assessment year, but the slab structure is the same, so this calculator applies to both years.

Can I switch between the old and new regime every year?

Salaried individuals without business income can choose either regime each year when filing the return, whatever they told their employer. A person with business or professional income must file Form 10-IEA before the return due date to opt out of the new regime, and can come back to the new regime only once; after that, they cannot opt out again.

Which deductions can I still claim under the new regime?

The standard deduction of ₹75,000 on salary or pension, the employer's contribution to NPS under Section 80CCD(2) up to 14% of basic salary plus dearness allowance, the family pension deduction of up to ₹25,000, and interest on a housing loan for a let-out property under Section 24(b), which can be set off only against that rental income. Section 80C, 80D, 80CCD(1B), HRA and home loan interest on a self-occupied house are available only in the old regime.

Is there a surcharge on high incomes?

Yes. A surcharge applies on income above ₹50 lakh: 10% between ₹50 lakh and ₹1 crore, 15% between ₹1 crore and ₹2 crore, and 25% between ₹2 crore and ₹5 crore. Above ₹5 crore the old regime charges 37% while the new regime caps the surcharge at 25%. Marginal relief applies at each threshold. This calculator shows the slab tax and 4% cess without surcharge.

Does this calculator work for senior citizens?

The calculator assumes a resident individual below 60. Under the old regime, the basic exemption is ₹3 lakh for people aged 60 to 79 and ₹5 lakh for people aged 80 or more, so their old regime tax will be lower than shown here. The new regime has the same slabs for every age group, so its result is unaffected.
